# A Hard Autumn: Written Documentation

## Part 1: Motivation and Medium

I have long been interested in how often the legitimacy of the Chinese Communist Party is discussed. Observers rarely ask the same question of other states. Democracies are assumed to have legitimacy built in, through elections. Many non-democratic states are assumed to rest on coercion, so analysts see no need to ask whether their rulers are legitimate. Commentary on China differs. It is common to say that the Party draws its legitimacy from economic performance, which treats legitimacy as something a ruler must keep earning. When I learned about the Mandate of Heaven in this course, I recognised the same logic in a much older form. A ruler's right to rule was not guaranteed, and it depended on how he treated the people. That suggested the question of legitimacy is a political and cultural inheritance rather than a modern invention, and I wanted to explore it.

I chose a game because it is a medium I know and enjoy, and because it fits the subject. Reading that a ruler had to keep earning his standing is different from having to decide, with too little money and uncertain information, whether to open the grain reserve or hold it back. A player who experiences these pressures first hand gets a better sense of what rulers in China worried about than a summary can give. The game is a browser-based text game in the tradition of *The Oregon Trail*. The player is the governor of a Chinese province through a drought, a winter and a spring. A fictional modern setting lets players meet the problems without needing a lot of background, and it makes the connection to the present direct. The vice-governor is the only recurring character, because I wanted the game to focus on the ideas rather than on a cast.

## Part 2: Core Takeaways

The game is openly judged by Mencius (4th century BCE). It is a modern adaptation, not a claim about how his ideas work in China today. I hope players take away five points.

1. **Rule was conditional.** Mencius tells King Xuan that a ruler who despoils humaneness and rightness is a "mere fellow", and that he has heard of the punishment of such a man but never of the killing of a ruler (1B8). The game never reveals a morality score, so players must judge for themselves what the people are owed.
2. **Livelihood came first.** For Mencius, securing food and safety was a duty of government, and punishing people driven to wrongdoing by want amounts to trapping them (1A7). In the game, punishing raiders from villages the relief never reached is recorded as a wrong, and so is holding back the grain reserve while people go hungry.
3. **Rule relied on officials who would say unwelcome things.** Mencius says a minister who finds the ruler at fault should remonstrate. If the ruler repeatedly does not listen, a minister of the ruler's own family should depose him, and a minister of another family should leave (5B9). In the game, Lin Wei raises specific matters. If you ignore him he resigns, and Beijing can remove you. Mencius places this duty with ministers close to the ruler, not with the people generally, and that distinction matters. The three-strikes rule and the inspection are my design, not Mencius's.
4. **Ideals were hard to practise.** A shortcut can work for a while: holding back the reserve leaves grain for the cold wave. A wrong is still a wrong when it works, and morality does not buy luck.
5. **There are two ways to measure a ruler, and they can disagree.** Beijing judges the province by its accounts and condition. Mencius would judge how the people were treated. The ending shows both verdicts, so a player can keep the books in order and still fail Mencius's standard, or the reverse.

The fifth point connects the game to my motivation. If legitimacy is argued today largely through performance, then the older argument, that standing depends on how the people are treated and that officials owe the ruler honest reporting, is still visible in how legitimacy is framed. I do not claim the game demonstrates that this continuity is real. It makes the old argument concrete so that players can judge for themselves how far it still applies.

My research was limited to the textual and encyclopedic sources below, and I did not examine the modern legitimacy literature directly. I considered Laozi, Mozi and Dong Zhongshu as alternative standards. I chose Mencius alone because I could not ground distinct, defensible verdicts for each school on the same decisions. The game labels each wrong as directly supported, inferred from the text, or my own game design.
