# A Hard Autumn: Written Documentation

## Part 1: Motivation and Medium

I have long been interested in how often the legitimacy of the Chinese Communist Party is discussed. Observers rarely ask the same question of other states. Democracies are assumed to have legitimacy built in, through elections. Many non-democratic states are assumed to rest on coercion, so analysts see no need to ask whether their rulers are legitimate. Commentary on China differs. It is common to say that the Party draws its legitimacy from economic performance, which treats legitimacy as something a ruler must keep earning. When I learned about the Mandate of Heaven in this course, I recognised the same logic in a much older form. A ruler's right to rule was not guaranteed, and it depended on how he treated the people. That suggested the question of legitimacy is a political and cultural inheritance rather than a modern invention, and I wanted to explore it.

I chose a game because it is a medium I know and enjoy, and because it fits the subject. Reading that a ruler had to keep earning his standing is different from having to decide, with too little money and uncertain information, whether to open the grain reserve or hold it back. A player who experiences these pressures first hand gets a better sense of what rulers in China worried about than a summary can give. The game is a browser-based text game in the tradition of *The Oregon Trail*. The player is the governor of a Chinese province through a drought, a winter and a spring. A fictional modern setting lets players meet the problems without needing a lot of background, and it makes the connection to the present direct. The vice-governor, Lin Wei — written as a devoted student of Mencius — is the only recurring character, because I wanted the game to focus on the ideas rather than on a cast.

## Part 2: Core Takeaway and Design

The central takeaway I want players to leave with is that **in the Chinese political tradition, legitimacy was conditional — and how you measure a ruler changes the verdict.** Mencius (4th century BCE) tells King Xuan that a ruler who despoils humaneness and rightness is no longer a ruler at all but a "mere fellow", and that he has heard of the punishment of such a man, never of the killing of a ruler (1B8). Standing is never permanent; it has to be earned. Today, however, commentary on China usually frames conditional legitimacy around economic performance and social order rather than moral conduct. The game puts those two standards side by side. At the end of the term, players receive two separate verdicts: Beijing judges the province on its accounts, stability and growth, while Mencius's standard judges how the people in their care were treated. Under real scarcity, those two measures pull apart. A player can cut corners, keep the books healthy, and earn a promotion from Beijing while failing Mencius's standard — or keep a clean record by Mencius's standard and be quietly sidelined by Beijing.

Rather than inventing an abstract morality score, I built Mencius's standard into the game's mechanics using two of his specific teachings:

- **Livelihood before punishment (1A7, 1B6, 3A3, 5A3).** For Mencius, securing people's food, safety and stable livelihood was the first duty of government; punishing people driven into wrongdoing by deprivation amounts to "trapping" them (1A7), and heavy exactions strip ordinary people of the constant livelihood on which moral steadiness depends (3A3). These passages define what counts as a wrong in play: withholding grain reserves while villages go hungry (1B6), arresting raiders from villages relief never reached (1A7), enforcing full tax quotas on drought-hit counties (3A3), handing relief logistics to an unreliable relative's firm — crossing the line sage-king Shun refused to cross in administering his brother's territory (5A3) — or staging a model village for a visiting official while poorer counties wait. Crucially, these shortcuts can improve the province's numbers, because in Mencius a wrong does not cease to be a wrong when it works.
- **Ministerial remonstrance (5B9).** Mencius places the duty to reprove a wayward ruler not on the public at large, but on ministers close to power: if the ruler repeatedly refuses to listen, a minister of another family should leave, and one of the ruler's own family should depose him (5B9). Lin Wei carries that role. Instead of showing a morality meter, the game has Lin confront the governor directly over specific wrongs. Ignoring him costs honest briefings and eventually drives him to resign, bringing an inspection from Beijing that can remove the governor from office. The three-strikes threshold and Beijing's inspection are my game design, not Mencius's.

This framing ties back to my original question. If legitimacy in China is argued today largely through performance, then the older argument — that standing depends on how the people are treated and that officials owe the ruler honest counsel — is still visible behind how legitimacy is debated, even when the two standards collide. I do not claim the game proves that this continuity is real; it makes the older argument concrete so that players can judge for themselves how far it still applies.

My research was limited to the textual and encyclopedic sources below, and I did not examine the modern legitimacy literature directly. I considered Laozi, Mozi and Dong Zhongshu as alternative standards, and chose Mencius alone because I could not ground distinct, defensible verdicts for each school on the same decisions. The ending screen's Notes & Sources tab documents the Mencius passages behind each matter raised in a run.
